# Abstract

Artificial intelligence matches or exceeds human performance on tasks with verifiable answers, from protein folding to Olympiad mathematics. Yet the capacity that governs scientific advance, judging which research ideas deserve pursuit before results are known, remains beyond AI's reach. This evaluative judgment is tacit, closer to taste than to reasoning: experts exercise it but cannot articulate it, and peer reviewers agree at barely above chance. Here we show that fine-tuning language models on institutional traces, decades of publication decisions in organisational psychology and management, recovers evaluative judgment inaccessible to both frontier AI and human expertise. Eleven frontier models, including GPT-5.2 High, Claude Opus 4.6, and Gemini 3.1 Pro, average 31.1% accuracy (chance = 25%) with balanced tier discrimination worse than random guessing. Journal editors and editorial board members reach 41.6%, outperforming frontier AI but far from ideal. Our fine-tuned ensemble achieves 60.8%, surpassing all frontier models and expert panels, while exhibiting calibrated self-knowledge that provides the strongest practically useful confidence signal among the highlighted AI systems. The institutional signal persists when training data lag the benchmark by five years, confirming durable evaluative structure rather than transient pattern matching. The missing piece in AI for science was not model scale but the right training signal — institutional traces learnable in as little as a single GPU-hour.
